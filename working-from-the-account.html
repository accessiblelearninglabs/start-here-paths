The request is yours. Here is what you are owed, and how to see it through.

One accommodation request, followed by the person who makes it, from the week it is written to the change it leaves behind. Seven stops; at each, the question in front of you, the resources that answer it, and what a good outcome looks like. It is a way into the landscape of seventy-three knowledge resources for anyone on the asking side of the desk, and a companion to the advisor’s path, which follows a request from the other side.

This path describes what the law and professional guidance say an institution owes, and how a request is commonly handled. It is not legal advice, and it does not replace an advisor, an advocate or legal counsel. Determinations in individual accommodation cases rest with accessibility advisors and accommodation offices.

  • Fifteen minutes

    Read the path through. It stands on its own and does not require a single link to be opened.

  • An hour

    Read the path, then open the stop you are at now, with its resources beside it.

  • A term

    Use the seven stops as a reading order through the landscape, from the first week to the last.

A note on confidentiality

The case that follows is not a real person’s. It is a composite of experiences common among learners who come to an accommodation office for the first time with a condition that began after they enrolled, written so that the request can be followed from beginning to end. No detail is drawn from a real learner, a real file or a real course.

Your own accommodation record is confidential. It is held by the office that creates it, disclosed only to the extent needed to deliver the accommodation, and protected by provincial privacy legislation (source 7). You can ask, at any point, who holds a record and who may see it, and the answer should be plain.

The case

Third year, a part-time job, a midterm in three weeks

You are in the third year of an arts degree and work fifteen hours a week. This term includes a seminar course with sixty to eighty pages of reading a week, a two-hour midterm of essay questions in week six, and a final exam. Your other courses are lighter, and you have been managing them.

In the spring of second year you had a concussion. You saw a doctor at the time and have the clinic’s note. Since then, reading for more than twenty minutes at a stretch has been difficult, and you have found that you lose what you read when you are working against the clock. Your grades in second year dropped, and you put it down to the job. You have never registered with the access office, because you did not think of what happened as a disability, and because you have no assessment that names anything.

In week three you look into getting one. The wait is months. The cost is more than you have. The midterm is in three weeks.

You write to the office anyway. What follows is the path from your side of the desk.

1What you are asking for

Week three. Before you write to the office.

The questionAm I asking the office to recognise a condition I cannot yet name, or to remove barriers I can describe?
What to read
  • Functional Impact in Academic Tasks — starts from what the course asks each week, not from a diagnosis. Sustained reading, timed writing, recall under pressure: this is where what changed for you and what the course requires actually meet, and it is the language an office works in.
  • Models of Disability as Design Decisions — the model implicit in how you read your own situation decides what you ask for. Read as a medical question, you have nothing to show. Read as an interaction between a change in you and a course built a certain way, you have a great deal.
  • Why Universal Design Never Eliminates Individual Accommodation — the anchor of the whole landscape. An individual request is not a failure on anyone’s part; it is the point at which something owed to you is met by skilled practice.
  • Access at Admission and Transition — the interval before the system knows a learner. You are in your third year, and to the accommodation system you are arriving now.
A good outcome looks like

Before you write, you write for yourself: what changed, when, and what it does to the tasks in front of you. Reading for a seminar used to take an evening and now takes three, with breaks. In a timed essay you lose the thread of a question you understood a minute earlier. You can describe what helps: shorter sessions, reading on a screen that reads aloud, more time. This is the frame the International Classification of Functioning supplies, in which functioning is the interaction of a health condition with activities, participation and the environment rather than a property of the person (source 17), and it is the frame disability studies has insisted on: a barrier is a feature of the environment and the institution, not a deficit in you (source 19).

Written that way, your account is not a substitute for evidence. It is evidence. Professional guidance for accommodation offices treats the learner’s own account as a primary source of information about barriers and about what has and has not worked, and holds that many requests can be decided with little outside documentation (source 14). The email you send asks for two things: a meeting, and interim arrangements for the midterm while anything longer is sorted out. It says what changed and what it does, attaches the clinic note, and does not apologise for not having an assessment.

If you are not sure this countsThe Human Rights Code names physical and mental disability as protected grounds and sets no requirement that a condition be permanent, visible or named by a specialist before the duty applies (source 8). Whether you are eligible for a particular accommodation is for the office to decide. Whether to ask is yours, and you do not have to settle the first question before you do.

2What you are owed

Week three. The email is sent. While you wait for a reply.

The questionWhat is the institution required to do, what am I expected to do, and what happens in the weeks before any assessment exists?
What to read
  • The Duty to Accommodate in Learning Design — the procedural duty and the substantive one, and accommodation to the point of undue hardship, in the Canadian frame. The first is a duty to run a fair process with you; the second is a duty to arrive at something that works.
  • The Case Law Behind Accommodation in Education — six decisions in plain language. Three carry this stop: Renaud, which makes the search for accommodation a shared responsibility in which you also take part (source 1); Meiorin, which places on the institution, not on you, the burden of showing that further accommodation would be undue hardship (source 2); and Moore, in which the service you are owed is described as meaningful access to education (source 3).
  • How a University Accommodation Policy Works — your institution’s own policy, read as the brief: who is eligible, who decides, what an instructor is responsible for, and how a disagreement is resolved. Read it before the meeting.
  • The Interactive Process in Learning Settings — what good faith requires on each side, and the points at which a process stalls: no response, an unworkable offer, or a request handled as a complaint.
  • Documentation Requirements and Medical Gatekeeping — what is actually required, set against what is routinely requested, and why the cost and waiting time of an assessment are barriers in their own right.
A good outcome looks like

The reply opens a process rather than issuing a ruling. It gives you a meeting date within the week, says what to bring, and says what the office can put in place for the midterm while the longer question is open. The duty it answers is the institution’s duty to accommodate under the Human Rights Code (source 8), and it is a positive duty: equal access to a public service can require the institution to take measures, not only to refrain from excluding (source 4). It calls for individual assessment rather than a blanket rule (source 5), with the focus on needs and limitations rather than on a diagnosis (source 9).

The weeks before an assessment are not a gap in the duty. Human rights guidance says that education providers have a duty to provide interim accommodation while an assessment is awaited, and that the lack of a formal assessment should not prevent a learner’s access to accommodation for their needs (source 9). Your part, under Renaud, is to take part in good faith: to describe what you need, to consider what is offered, and to bring the request forward in time for it to be met (source 1), which you have done by writing in week three rather than the week of the midterm.

If the reply says to come back with an assessmentAsk, in writing, what interim arrangements the office can make for the midterm in the meantime, what documentation it would consider sufficient, and whether the institution has a fund or bursary for assessments. Each of those questions has an answer the office should be able to give, and the guidance its profession publishes says that documentation is sought when a learner cannot explain how the disability creates the barrier and how the accommodation would address it, that blanket rules about the age or type of documentation are not applied, and that documentation processes must not be burdensome or have the effect of discouraging learners from seeking accommodation (source 14). You can explain the barrier and the accommodation. Say so.

3Reading the course before you ask

Week four. Before the meeting, with the course outline open.

The questionWhich of these barriers is a design choice the course could have made differently, and which is mine to carry into the room?
What to read
  • Time, Pacing and Deadlines as Design Variables — a time limit is either part of what an assessment measures or a convention it inherited. A two-hour essay midterm is almost always the second, and knowing that changes how you ask.
  • Accessible Documents as Everyday Practice — whether the readings on the course site are structured documents that a screen can read aloud, or scanned images that nothing can. The difference decides whether read-aloud is available to you this week.
  • Assistive Technology and Adaptive Strategies — what exists and what it does: text-to-speech, reading modes that shorten line length and raise contrast, and the tools already built into the devices you own. Some of this is yours today, with no letter.
  • Designing Out the Disclosure Requirement — a reading that only works with a disclosure and a letter is a design choice; a reading that works for anyone is not.
  • Essential Requirements and the Limits of Flexibility — what a course genuinely requires, separated from what it has inherited by convention. The seminar requires that you read closely and argue in writing. It does not require that you do so at a fixed speed.
A good outcome looks like

You go into the meeting with the course sorted into three piles. Things you can change today: the readings that are proper text can be read aloud by free software on your own device, and you have tried it. Things the course could have done for everyone: three of the weekly readings are scanned images that no software can read, and that is a barrier for anyone who uses read-aloud, not only you; a two-hour limit on an essay exam measures speed as well as argument. Things that are owed to you individually: more time on the midterm, and perhaps the readings in a usable format where the course cannot supply them.

The design findings belong to the framework they come from. Readings in a form that can be read aloud are a matter of representation in the Universal Design for Learning Guidelines; time on an assessment is a matter of action and expression (source 20). Variability is the rule in any class, not the exception (source 21), which is why the fix for your barrier is usually a fix for someone else’s. It is not a fix for everyone: design reduces the individual duty without discharging it (source 22), and the part that remains is the part you are about to ask for.

Decide, too, what you will say to the instructor and when. Asking for readings in a usable format is not an accommodation request and needs no disclosure; it is a request that the course meet an accessibility standard it is already expected to meet (source 26). You can make it this week, from any account, without mentioning the office or anything about yourself.

4The meeting

Week four. You go in.

The questionWhat do I need to bring, what can I ask, and what can I decline?
What to read
  • The Advising Conversation — what a well-designed conversation looks like from the advisor’s side, so that you recognise one, and recognise when one is not happening.
  • Reading Disability Documentation — what a report can establish and what it cannot; the weight professional guidance gives to your own account; and what a clinic note from the time of an injury does and does not say.
  • Funding Routes for Disability-Related Supports and Equipment — cost as an access barrier: what an assessment costs, what the institution and the student aid system can pay toward it, and in what order.
  • Responding to Distress and Making a Referral — what the person across the desk is trained to do if the conversation is hard, and what they are not.
  • Intersectionality in Accommodation: Who Reaches the Office — the learners who never reach this room, and why: cost, time, not knowing the office exists, not thinking of what happened as a disability. You nearly did not. Knowing that is part of the seventh stop.
A good outcome looks like

You bring four things: your written account from the first stop; the clinic note; the course outline with your three piles marked; and your questions. The advisor tells you at the outset what will be asked, why, who will see it and what happens next, and does not ask you to establish what happened again from the beginning. That is what a conversation built on the principles of a trauma-informed approach looks like: safety, transparency, collaboration, voice and choice (source 18). If it is not what you meet, you can ask for it in those terms.

The clinic note is read for what it establishes, that an injury occurred and when, and not for what it cannot, which is how reading and timed writing meet this seminar this term. Your account does that work, and it is weighed for its clarity and its consistency with the note and the outline rather than for the absence of a report (source 14). You ask three things plainly: what interim arrangements the office will put in place for the midterm; what documentation it would consider sufficient for the longer term, and whether your account and the note might be; and how an assessment could be paid for. On the last, there is more than one route. The institution may hold a bursary for assessments, administered through the office; the federal student grant for services and equipment can reimburse the cost of an assessment, to a stated maximum, once there is a receipt and documentation of the disability (sources 27, 28). The order matters: the bursary comes before the assessment, and the reimbursement after. Ask which the institution has.

You can decline things. You do not have to describe the injury in more detail than you choose, name any treatment, or agree to an accommodation you do not think will work. You can ask who will see the record and what it will say, and you can ask for the reasons for any decision in writing (sources 7, 9).

If you are told the account is not enoughMany offices would say so, and the guidance their own profession publishes says otherwise: the learner is a primary source, third-party documentation is sought when the learner cannot explain the barrier and the accommodation, and blanket age limits on documentation are not applied (source 14). Human rights guidance adds that an office should consider whether it can waive a requirement for a professional assessment, and that non-medical evidence can inform an accommodation decision (source 9). Ask for the reason in writing, ask what interim arrangement will be made in the meantime, and keep the reply. It is the record of the process, and the process is what the institution is accountable for.

5Deciding what to accept

Week five. The office offers interim extended time on the midterm, pending an assessment, and says that the format of the readings is an academic matter for the instructor.

The questionDoes the offer remove the barrier I described, what do I say to the instructor, and what do I do about the part the office has handed back?
What to read
  • Accommodated Exams and Assessment Logistics — what extended time changes and what it does not; what the research on it shows; and what happens if something goes wrong during the exam itself.
  • Eliminating Construct-Irrelevant Barriers in Assessment — the validity principle: a mark should reflect what you can do, not the obstacles in the way of showing it. This is the argument for extended time, in the office’s own language.
  • Essential Requirements and the Limits of Flexibility — read again, for the readings: a seminar requires close reading. It does not require reading from a scanned image.
  • Working with Accommodation Offices and Access Advisors — what the office decides, what the instructor decides, and what falls between them. The readings fell between them. That is where you go next.
  • Peer Mentorship as Access Infrastructure — the middle ground between a learner and an office. A learner who has asked an instructor for usable readings before knows how the conversation goes.
A good outcome looks like

You take the extended time, and you ask one question about it: how was the amount decided? A pan-Canadian study found the common allowance of one and a half times usually set at intake from policy or custom rather than from anything about the person (source 10), and the research on extended time supports individual decisions over standing formulas (source 11). If the answer is “that is what we give”, you can say what you described in the first stop and ask whether the amount reflects it. The accommodation changes the conditions of the exam, not what it measures; a time limit that is not part of what an assessment measures is construct-irrelevant variance, and removing it is what validity requires (sources 12, 13).

The readings are a different kind of request, and you make it to the instructor directly, this week. It is not an accommodation request and you do not describe yourself in it: three of the readings are scanned images that cannot be read by software, and you ask whether text versions can be posted. Most instructors do not know which of their files are images, and most will fix it when told. If the instructor cannot, the office’s alternate-format service can produce them, and that is an accommodation the office does decide. Either way, the readings arrive in a form you can use before the midterm.

If the answer is noA refusal has to have a reason, and the reason has to be that the accommodation would alter what the course measures or impose undue hardship. The institution carries the burden of showing that (source 2); you do not carry the burden of disproving it. Effort can choose between two accommodations that both work; it cannot defeat the only one that does, short of undue hardship (source 6). Ask for the reason in writing, and go to the sixth stop.

6Making sure it is real

Weeks five and six. The interim arrangement is agreed; the midterm is next week.

The questionHow do I know the accommodation exists before I need it, and what do I do if it does not?
What to read
  • Working with Accommodation Offices and Access Advisors — the implementation gap, in which an approved accommodation is recorded and then not delivered. Knowing it has a name helps you ask about it without apology.
  • Delivering Accommodated Exams in a Learning Platform — if the midterm is online, the extended time has to be set in the platform for you, and checked; a test run with your own set-up catches it.
  • Adapting Test Papers to Alternate Formats — if you asked for the paper in a different form, it is produced before the day and not on it.
  • Training and Supervising Student Access Staff — the invigilator who is in the room on the day, and what they should already know before you arrive.
  • Funding Routes for Disability-Related Supports and Equipment — read again for the assessment: the application, the receipt, and the order in which the money moves.
A good outcome looks like

Every line of the arrangement has an owner and a date, and you can ask for both. You confirm with the exam service, a week out, that the midterm is booked with the extended time and the room you were told. You check the course site for the text versions of the readings so that you do not have to ask twice. You know who to speak to if the time is not set on the day, and you have the email that says what was agreed. Keep a copy of everything. An accommodation that is recorded and not delivered has discharged nothing (source 15), and it is reasonable to say so.

The assessment runs on its own track. If the institution has a bursary, the application goes in now, through the office, so that the wait begins; if the route is reimbursement, you keep the receipt and the documentation it will ask for (source 28). The interim arrangement does not expire while you wait. If it is said to, ask, in writing, on what basis.

If the process stallsNo reply in a reasonable time, an offer that does not work, or a request treated as a complaint: each is a recognised failure of the process rather than a verdict on you. Ask, in writing, for a decision and the reason for it. Your institution’s accommodation policy sets out how a disagreement is resolved, and most institutions also have an ombudsperson and a student association with advocacy staff who can accompany you. Outside the institution, the British Columbia Human Rights Tribunal hears complaints under the Human Rights Code, and a complaint must ordinarily be filed within one year of the conduct (source 8). That route exists so that the duty is real; knowing it is there changes how the earlier conversations go. This is information, not advice, and an advocate or legal counsel is the right person to ask before taking it.

7For the next person

December. You sat the midterm with the time you needed, the readings arrived as text from week five, and the assessment is booked for the spring through the bursary.

The questionWhat did this request show about the course and the service, and what can I do so that the next person does not have to ask?
What to read
  • Documentation Requirements and Medical Gatekeeping — read again, now as the thing to change: an office that treats the account as evidence, holds an assessment fund, and applies no blanket rules is an office the next person can reach.
  • Professional Standards for Disability Services — a service is expected to evaluate itself against professional standards, and disabled learners are meant to take part in that self-study (sources 15, 16). Your account of the process is evidence the service needs.
  • Peer Mentorship as Access Infrastructure — the middle ground between a learner and an office, where a learner who has been through it is the person best placed to stand.
  • Practising from Inside the Population — the principle that research and practice on disability should be set and governed by disabled people. The seventh stop is where that principle becomes something you do.
  • Disability Justice and Where It Came From — a framework with named authors: leadership by those most affected, and the collective rather than individual answer to a system that asks each person to prove themselves alone.
  • Intersectionality in Accommodation: Who Reaches the Office — read again, for the people who did not write in week three. The structural reasons they did not are what the service can change.
A good outcome looks like

Three things you can do, none of them required. For the instructor: say what would have worked without a request. Readings posted as text rather than images would have met one of your needs with no letter, no office and no disclosure, and would have served everyone in the seminar who reads on a screen. That is a design note, not a complaint, and instructors act on it more often than they are given credit for.

For the service: tell it what the process was like. How long the reply took, whether the interim arrangement held, how the amount of time was decided, what it was like to be asked for an assessment you could not afford. A service measures itself by evidence of this kind, and a pattern across learners is what it acts on (source 15). The changes your case points to are the ones its own professional guidance already names: the learner’s account treated as a primary source, no blanket rules about the age of documentation, and a process that is not burdensome and does not discourage anyone from asking (source 14); and the ones human rights guidance names: interim accommodation as a duty, and the lack of a formal assessment never the reason access is withheld (source 9). An assessment fund is a lever the institution can pull this year. Say so.

For the people after you: a learner who has been through this holds knowledge the office cannot write down, which is why peer mentorship exists and why the disability justice tradition puts leadership with those most affected (source 23). The seven stops you have followed map onto the four components the self-advocacy research names: knowledge of self, knowledge of rights, communication, and leadership (source 24). The first six stops are the first three. This stop is the fourth, and the research on learners with disabilities in post-secondary education finds that self-determination of this kind is among the things that most shape how they fare (source 25). A request handled well is yours. Spoken about, it becomes the case for the next one.
