7Changing the institution

December. Avery sat the midterm with the time that was agreed, the readings arrived as text from week five, and the assessment is booked for the spring through the bursary.

The questionWhat did this one request show about the course and the service, and what can a learner do so that the next person does not have to ask?
What to read
  • Documentation Requirements and Medical Gatekeeping — read again, now as the thing to change: an office that treats the account as evidence, holds an assessment fund, and sets no blanket rules is an office the next person can reach.
  • Professional Standards for Disability Services — a service is expected to evaluate itself against professional standards, and disabled learners are meant to take part in that self-study (sources 15, 16). A learner’s account of the process is evidence the service needs.
  • Peer Mentorship as Access Infrastructure — the middle ground between a learner and an office, where a learner who has been through it is the person best placed to stand.
  • Practising from Inside the Population — the principle that research and practice on disability should be set and governed by disabled people. The seventh stop is where that principle becomes something a learner does.
  • Disability Justice and Where It Came From — a framework with named authors: leadership by those most affected, and a collective rather than individual answer to a system that asks each person to prove themselves alone.
  • Intersectionality in Accommodation: Who Reaches the Office — read again, for the people who did not write in week three. The structural reasons they did not are what the service can change.
A good decision looks like

Three things Avery can do, none of them required. For the instructor: say what would have worked without a request. Readings posted as text rather than images would have met one need with no letter, no office, and no disclosure, and would have served everyone in the seminar who reads on a screen. That is a design note, not a complaint.

For the service: say what the process was like. How long the reply took, whether the interim arrangement held, how the amount of time was decided, what it was like to be asked about an assessment that could not be afforded. A service measures itself by evidence of this kind, and a pattern across learners is what it acts on (source 15). The changes Avery’s case points to are the ones the service’s own professional guidance already names: the learner’s report treated as a source that may be sufficient, no blanket limits on the age of documentation, and a process that is not burdensome and does not discourage anyone from asking (source 14); and the ones human rights guidance names: interim accommodation as a duty, and the lack of a formal assessment never the reason access is withheld (source 9). An assessment fund is a lever the institution can pull this year.

For the people after Avery: a learner who has been through this holds knowledge the office cannot write down, which is why peer mentorship exists, why the longer tradition holds that research and practice on disability should be governed by disabled people (source 23), and why the disability justice tradition puts leadership with those most affected (source 24). The seven stops Avery followed map onto the four components the self-advocacy research names: knowledge of self, knowledge of rights, communication, and leadership (source 25). The first six stops exercise the first three; this stop is the fourth, and the research on learners with disabilities in post-secondary education finds that self-determination of this kind is among the things that most shape how they fare (source 26). A request handled well is the learner’s. Spoken about, it becomes the case for the next one.
