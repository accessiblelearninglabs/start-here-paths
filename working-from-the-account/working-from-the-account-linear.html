Accessible Learning Labs · Start here path · Working from the account · The participant’s path

The system wants a history. The account is evidence.

The participant is the person seeking to take part, and the one who knows their own access best. This path follows one, Avery, a learner, and one accommodation request from the week Avery writes it to the change it leaves behind. Seven stops; at each, the question the participant faces, the resources that answer it, and what a good decision looks like. A patient or an employee meets the same questions at different milestones. It is a way into the landscape of seventy-three knowledge resources for anyone on the asking side of the desk, and a companion to the practitioner’s path, which follows a request from the other side.

This path describes what the law and professional guidance say an institution owes, and how a request is commonly handled. It is not legal advice, and it does not replace an advisor, an advocate or legal counsel. Determinations in individual accommodation cases rest with accessibility advisors and accommodation offices.

Screen reader and keyboard optimised version. All content is presented in reading order, with no tabs, frames or scripting. Every stop is a section with its own heading, so the page can be navigated by heading; the sources are numbered and each number in the text links to its entry. An interactive version presents the same content in panels.

Three ways to read this

  • Fifteen minutes. Read the path through. It stands on its own and does not require a single link to be opened.
  • An hour. Read the path, then open the stop that matches where you are, with its resources beside it.
  • A term. Use the seven stops as a reading order through the landscape, from the first week to the last.

A note on confidentiality

Avery is not a real person. The case that follows is a composite of experiences common among learners who come to an accommodation office for the first time with a change that began after they enrolled, written so that a request can be followed from beginning to end. No detail is drawn from a real learner, a real file or a real course.

An accommodation record is confidential. It is held by the office that creates it, disclosed only to the extent needed to deliver the accommodation, and protected by provincial privacy legislation (source 7). A learner can ask, at any point, who holds a record and who may see it, and the answer should be plain. A real case is never discussed, taught from or written about in a form that could identify the person, and the same applies to anyone who uses this path as a model.

The case

The case: Avery, third year, a part-time job, a midterm in three weeks

Avery is in the third year of an arts degree and works fifteen hours a week. This term includes a seminar course with sixty to eighty pages of reading a week, a two-hour midterm of essay questions in week six, and a final exam. The other courses are lighter, and Avery has been managing them.

Since the spring of second year, reading for more than twenty minutes at a stretch has been difficult for Avery, and what was read tends to slip away when the clock is running. Avery saw a doctor when it began and has the clinic’s note from that visit. Grades dropped in second year, and Avery put it down to the job. Avery has never registered with the access office, because what changed did not seem like the kind of thing the office was for, and because there is no assessment that names anything.

In week three, Avery looks into getting one. The wait is months. The cost is more than Avery has. The midterm is in three weeks.

Avery writes to the office anyway. What follows is the work, from the side of the desk where the request is made.

Stop 1 of 7

Stop 1: The frame

Where the request is now

Week three. Before the email to the office is written.

The question

Is this a request for the office to recognise something that has no name yet, or a request to remove barriers that can be described? And what is Avery’s part in it?

What to read

  • Functional Impact in Academic Tasks — starts from what the course asks each week, not from a diagnosis. Sustained reading, timed writing, recall under pressure: this is where what changed for Avery and what the seminar requires actually meet, and it is the language an office works in.
  • Disability Categories and the Barriers People Meet — the reference layer, written barrier-first. A way to describe what sustained reading and timed writing now ask of Avery, without needing a name for why.
  • Models of Disability as Design Decisions — the model implicit in how a learner reads their own situation decides what they ask for. Read as a medical question, Avery has nothing to show. Read as an interaction between a change in a person and a course built a certain way, Avery has a great deal.
  • Why Universal Design Never Eliminates Individual Accommodation — the anchor of the whole landscape. An individual request is not a failure on anyone’s part; it is the point at which something owed to a person is met by skilled practice.
  • Access at Admission and Transition — the interval before the system knows a learner. Avery is in third year, and to the accommodation system Avery is arriving now.

A good decision looks like

Before writing to the office, Avery writes for Avery: what changed, when, and what it does to the tasks in front of them. Reading for the seminar used to take an evening and now takes three, with breaks. In a timed essay, the thread of a question understood a minute earlier is gone. Avery can also say what helps: shorter sessions, text that a device reads aloud, more time. This is the frame the International Classification of Functioning supplies, in which functioning is the interaction of a health condition with activities, participation and the environment rather than a property of the person (source 17), and it is the frame disability studies has long insisted on: a barrier is a feature of the environment and the institution, not a deficit in the learner (source 19).

Written that way, the account is not a substitute for evidence. It is evidence. Professional guidance for accommodation offices treats the learner’s own report as a source that may be sufficient on its own to establish a need for accommodation, and holds that a report that is rich, consistent and detailed may be the most valuable source of information there is (source 14). The email Avery sends asks for two things: a meeting, and interim arrangements for the midterm while anything longer is worked out. It says what changed and what it does, attaches the clinic note, and does not apologise for the absence of an assessment.

Avery’s part is named at the same time. Avery decides what to disclose, to whom, and when; whether to accept what is offered; and whether to say, afterwards, what the process was like. Avery does not decide eligibility, and does not have to. Keeping those apart is what makes the rest of the path possible.

Where practice disagreesWhether a change that has no name yet counts. Many learners in Avery’s position do not write, because they have decided the answer in advance. The Human Rights Code names physical and mental disability as protected grounds and sets no requirement that a condition be permanent, visible or named by a specialist before the duty applies (source 8). Whether Avery is eligible for a particular accommodation is for the office to decide. Whether to ask is Avery’s, and the first question does not have to be settled before the second.

Stop 2 of 7

Stop 2: What is owed

Where the request is now

Week three. The email is sent. While Avery waits for a reply.

The question

What is the institution required to do, what is Avery expected to do, and what happens in the weeks before any assessment exists?

What to read

  • The Duty to Accommodate in Learning Design — the procedural duty and the substantive one, and accommodation to the point of undue hardship, in the Canadian frame. The first is a duty to run a fair process with the learner; the second is a duty to arrive at something that works.
  • The Case Law Behind Accommodation in Education — six decisions in plain language. Three carry this stop: Renaud, which makes the search for accommodation a shared responsibility in which the learner also takes part (source 1); Meiorin, which places on the institution, not the learner, the burden of showing that further accommodation would be undue hardship (source 2); and Moore, in which the service owed is described as meaningful access to education (source 3).
  • How a University Accommodation Policy Works — the institution’s own policy, read as the brief: who is eligible, who decides, what an instructor is responsible for, and how a disagreement is resolved. Read before the meeting, it changes what is asked in it.
  • The Interactive Process in Learning Settings — what good faith requires on each side, and the points at which a process stalls: no response, an unworkable offer, or a request handled as a complaint.
  • Documentation Requirements and Medical Gatekeeping — what is actually required, set against what is routinely requested, and why the cost and waiting time of an assessment are barriers in their own right.

A good decision looks like

The reply opens a process rather than issuing a ruling. It gives Avery a meeting date within the week, says what to bring, and says what the office can put in place for the midterm while the longer question is open. The duty it answers is the institution’s duty to accommodate under the Human Rights Code (source 8), and it is a positive duty: equal access to a public service can require the institution to take measures, not only to refrain from excluding (source 4). It calls for individual assessment rather than a blanket rule (source 5), with the focus on needs and limitations rather than on a diagnosis (source 9).

The weeks before an assessment are not a gap in the duty. Human rights guidance says that education providers have a duty to provide interim accommodation while an assessment is awaited, and that the lack of a formal assessment should not prevent a learner’s access to accommodation for their needs (source 9). Avery’s part, under Renaud, is to take part in good faith: to describe the need, to consider what is offered, and to bring the request forward in time for it to be met (source 1), which writing in week three rather than the week of the midterm has done.

Where practice disagreesWhether an office may reply, in effect, “come back with an assessment.” Some do. This path holds that the reply Avery is owed answers three questions instead: what interim arrangements will be made for the midterm; what documentation the office would consider sufficient; and whether the institution has a fund or bursary for assessments. The guidance the profession publishes says that third-party documentation is sought when the learner cannot clearly connect the disability to the barrier and the accommodation, that blanket limits on the age of documentation are not set, and that a documentation process must not be burdensome or have the effect of discouraging learners from seeking accommodation (source 14). Avery can make the connection. If the reply does not ask those three questions, Avery asks them, in writing.

Stop 3 of 7

Stop 3: Before anyone asks

Where the request is now

Week four. Before the meeting, with the course outline open.

The question

Which of these barriers is a design choice the course could have made differently, and which is Avery’s to carry into the room?

What to read

  • Time, Pacing and Deadlines as Design Variables — a time limit is either part of what an assessment measures or a convention it inherited. A two-hour essay midterm is almost always the second, and knowing that changes how the request is made.
  • Accessible Documents as Everyday Practice — whether the readings on the course site are structured documents that a device can read aloud, or scanned images that nothing can. The difference decides whether read-aloud is available this week.
  • Assistive Technology and Adaptive Strategies — what exists and what it does: text-to-speech, reading modes that shorten line length and raise contrast, and the tools already built into the devices a learner owns. Some of this is available today, with no letter.
  • Designing Out the Disclosure Requirement — a reading that only works with a disclosure and a letter is a design choice; a reading that works for anyone is not.
  • Essential Requirements and the Limits of Flexibility — what a course genuinely requires, separated from what it has inherited by convention. The seminar requires close reading and written argument. It does not require that either happen at a fixed speed.

A good decision looks like

Avery goes into the meeting with the course sorted into three piles. Things Avery can change today: the readings that are proper text can be read aloud by free software on Avery’s own device, and Avery has tried it. Things the course could have done for everyone: three of the weekly readings are scanned images that no software can read, which is a barrier for anyone who uses read-aloud, not only Avery; and a two-hour limit on an essay exam measures speed as well as argument. Things that are owed individually: more time on the midterm, and the readings in a usable format where the course cannot supply them.

The design findings belong to the framework they come from. Readings in a form that can be read aloud are a matter of representation in the Universal Design for Learning Guidelines; time on an assessment is a matter of action and expression (source 20). Variability is the rule in any class, not the exception (source 21), which is why the fix for Avery’s barrier is usually a fix for someone else’s. It is not a fix for everyone: design reduces the individual duty without discharging it (source 22), and the part that remains is the part Avery is about to ask for.

Avery also decides what to say to the instructor, and when. Asking for readings in a usable format is not an accommodation request and needs no disclosure; it is a request that the course meet an accessibility standard it is already expected to meet (source 27). Avery can make it this week, from any account, without mentioning the office or anything personal.

Stop 4 of 7

Stop 4: When someone asks

Where the request is now

Week four. Avery goes in.

The question

What does Avery need to bring, what can Avery ask, and what can Avery decline?

What to read

  • The Advising Conversation — what a well-designed conversation looks like from the advisor’s side, so that a learner recognises one, and recognises when one is not happening.
  • Reading Disability Documentation — what a report can establish and what it cannot; the weight professional guidance gives to the learner’s own account; and what a clinic note from the time a change began does and does not say.
  • Funding Routes for Disability-Related Supports and Equipment — cost as an access barrier: what an assessment costs, what the institution and the student aid system can pay toward it, and in what order.
  • Responding to Distress and Making a Referral — what the person across the desk is trained to do if the conversation is hard, and what they are not.
  • Intersectionality in Accommodation: Who Reaches the Office — the learners who never reach this room, and why: cost, time, not knowing the office exists, not thinking of what changed as the kind of thing the office is for. Avery nearly did not. Knowing that is part of the seventh stop.

A good decision looks like

Avery brings four things: the written account from the first stop; the clinic note; the course outline with the three piles marked; and a list of questions. The advisor says at the outset what will be asked, why, who will see it and what happens next, and does not ask Avery to establish from the beginning what has already been written down. That is what a conversation built on the principles of a trauma-informed approach looks like: safety, transparency, collaboration, voice and choice (source 18). If it is not what Avery meets, Avery can ask for it in those terms.

The clinic note is read for what it establishes, that something changed and when, and not for what it cannot, which is how reading and timed writing meet this seminar this term. Avery’s account does that work, and it is weighed for its clarity and its consistency with the note and the outline rather than for the absence of a report; an experienced advisor’s own observations in the room are a recognised form of documentation too (source 14). Avery asks three things plainly: what interim arrangements the office will put in place for the midterm; what documentation it would consider sufficient for the longer term, and whether the account and the note might be; and how an assessment could be paid for. On the last, there is more than one route. The institution may hold a bursary for assessments, administered through the office; the federal student grant for services and equipment can reimburse the cost of an assessment, to a stated maximum, once there is a receipt and documentation of the disability (sources 28, 29). The order matters: the bursary comes before the assessment, and the reimbursement after. Avery asks which the institution has.

Avery can decline things. Avery does not have to describe what changed in more detail than Avery chooses, name any treatment, or agree to an accommodation that does not seem likely to work. Avery can ask who will see the record and what it will say, and can ask for the reasons for any decision in writing (sources 7, 9).

Where practice disagreesWhether the account is enough. Many offices would say it is not, and the guidance their own profession publishes says otherwise: the learner’s report may be sufficient on its own, third-party documentation is sought when the learner cannot make the connection between disability, barrier and accommodation, and blanket limits on the age of documentation are not set (source 14). Human rights guidance adds that an office should consider whether it can waive a requirement for a professional assessment, and that evidence from non-medical sources can inform an accommodation decision (source 9). If Avery is told the account is not enough, Avery asks for the reason in writing, asks what interim arrangement will be made in the meantime, and keeps the reply. It is the record of the process, and the process is what the institution is accountable for.

Stop 5 of 7

Stop 5: The hard call

Where the request is now

Week five. The office offers interim extended time on the midterm, pending an assessment, and says that the format of the readings is an academic matter for the instructor.

The question

Does the offer remove the barrier Avery described, what does Avery say to the instructor, and what happens to the part the office has handed back?

What to read

  • Accommodated Exams and Assessment Logistics — what extended time changes and what it does not; what the research on it shows; and what happens if something goes wrong during the exam itself.
  • Eliminating Construct-Irrelevant Barriers in Assessment — the validity principle: a mark should reflect what a person can do, not the obstacles in the way of showing it. This is the argument for extended time, in the office’s own language.
  • Essential Requirements and the Limits of Flexibility — read again, for the readings: a seminar requires close reading. It does not require reading from a scanned image.
  • Working with Accommodation Offices and Access Advisors — what the office decides, what the instructor decides, and what falls between them. The readings fell between them. That is where Avery goes next.
  • Peer Mentorship as Access Infrastructure — the middle ground between a learner and an office. A learner who has asked an instructor for usable readings before knows how the conversation goes.

A good decision looks like

Avery accepts the extended time, and asks one question about it: how was the amount decided? A pan-Canadian study found the common allowance of one and a half times usually set at intake from policy or custom rather than from anything about the person (source 10), and the research on extended time supports individual decisions over standing formulas (source 11). If the answer is “that is what we give,” Avery returns to the account from the first stop and asks whether the amount reflects it. The accommodation changes the conditions of the exam, not what it measures; a time limit that is not part of what an assessment measures is construct-irrelevant variance, and removing it is what validity requires (sources 12, 13).

The readings are a different kind of request, and Avery makes it to the instructor directly, the same week. It is not an accommodation request and Avery does not describe anything personal in it: three of the readings are scanned images that software cannot read, and Avery asks whether text versions can be posted. Most instructors do not know which of their files are images, and most will fix it when told. If the instructor cannot, the office’s alternate-format service can produce them, and that is an accommodation the office does decide. Either way, the readings arrive in a form Avery can use before the midterm.

Where practice disagreesWhether a learner should approach an instructor at all while a request is open with the office. Some advise waiting for the letter so that everything comes through one channel. This path holds that a request for usable readings is the course’s own business, owed to everyone, and that waiting would make a design failure into Avery’s accommodation. If any answer is no, it has to have a reason, and the reason has to be that the change would alter what the course measures or impose undue hardship. The institution carries the burden of showing that (source 2); the learner does not carry the burden of disproving it. Effort can choose between two accommodations that both work; it cannot defeat the only one that does, short of undue hardship (source 6). Avery asks for the reason in writing, and goes to the sixth stop.

Stop 6 of 7

Stop 6: Making it real

Where the request is now

Weeks five and six. The interim arrangement is agreed; the midterm is next week.

The question

How does Avery know the accommodation exists before it is needed, and what happens if it does not?

What to read

  • Working with Accommodation Offices and Access Advisors — the implementation gap, in which an approved accommodation is recorded and then not delivered. Knowing it has a name makes it possible to ask about without apology.
  • Delivering Accommodated Exams in a Learning Platform — if the midterm is online, the extended time has to be set in the platform for the right person, and checked; a test run with the learner’s own set-up catches it.
  • Adapting Test Papers to Alternate Formats — if the paper was requested in a different form, it is produced before the day and not on it.
  • Training and Supervising Student Access Staff — the invigilator who is in the room on the day, and what they should already know before the learner arrives.
  • Funding Routes for Disability-Related Supports and Equipment — read again for the assessment: the application, the receipt, and the order in which the money moves.

A good decision looks like

Every line of the arrangement has an owner and a date, and Avery can ask for both. A week out, Avery confirms with the exam service that the midterm is booked with the extended time and in the room that was named. Avery checks the course site for the text versions of the readings, so that no one has to be asked twice. Avery knows who to speak to if the time is not set on the day, and has the email that says what was agreed. Avery keeps a copy of everything. An accommodation that is recorded and not delivered has discharged nothing (source 15), and it is reasonable to say so.

The assessment runs on its own track. If the institution has a bursary, the application goes in now, through the office, so that the wait begins; if the route is reimbursement, Avery keeps the receipt and the documentation it will ask for (source 29). The interim arrangement does not expire while the wait runs. If it is said to, Avery asks, in writing, on what basis.

If the process stallsNo reply in a reasonable time, an offer that does not work, or a request treated as a complaint: each is a recognised failure of the process rather than a verdict on the person. The learner asks, in writing, for a decision and the reason for it. The institution’s accommodation policy sets out how a disagreement is resolved, and most institutions also have an ombudsperson and a student association with advocacy staff who can accompany a learner. Outside the institution, the British Columbia Human Rights Tribunal hears complaints under the Human Rights Code, and a complaint must ordinarily be filed within one year of the conduct (source 8). That route exists so that the duty is real; knowing it is there changes how the earlier conversations go. This is information, not advice, and an advocate or legal counsel is the right person to ask before taking it.

Stop 7 of 7

Stop 7: Changing the institution

Where the request is now

December. Avery sat the midterm with the time that was agreed, the readings arrived as text from week five, and the assessment is booked for the spring through the bursary.

The question

What did this one request show about the course and the service, and what can a learner do so that the next person does not have to ask?

What to read

  • Documentation Requirements and Medical Gatekeeping — read again, now as the thing to change: an office that treats the account as evidence, holds an assessment fund and sets no blanket rules is an office the next person can reach.
  • Professional Standards for Disability Services — a service is expected to evaluate itself against professional standards, and disabled learners are meant to take part in that self-study (sources 15, 16). A learner’s account of the process is evidence the service needs.
  • Peer Mentorship as Access Infrastructure — the middle ground between a learner and an office, where a learner who has been through it is the person best placed to stand.
  • Practising from Inside the Population — the principle that research and practice on disability should be set and governed by disabled people. The seventh stop is where that principle becomes something a learner does.
  • Disability Justice and Where It Came From — a framework with named authors: leadership by those most affected, and a collective rather than individual answer to a system that asks each person to prove themselves alone.
  • Intersectionality in Accommodation: Who Reaches the Office — read again, for the people who did not write in week three. The structural reasons they did not are what the service can change.

A good decision looks like

Three things Avery can do, none of them required. For the instructor: say what would have worked without a request. Readings posted as text rather than images would have met one need with no letter, no office and no disclosure, and would have served everyone in the seminar who reads on a screen. That is a design note, not a complaint, and instructors act on it more often than they are given credit for.

For the service: say what the process was like. How long the reply took, whether the interim arrangement held, how the amount of time was decided, what it was like to be asked about an assessment that could not be afforded. A service measures itself by evidence of this kind, and a pattern across learners is what it acts on (source 15). The changes Avery’s case points to are the ones the service’s own professional guidance already names: the learner’s report treated as a source that may be sufficient, no blanket limits on the age of documentation, and a process that is not burdensome and does not discourage anyone from asking (source 14); and the ones human rights guidance names: interim accommodation as a duty, and the lack of a formal assessment never the reason access is withheld (source 9). An assessment fund is a lever the institution can pull this year.

For the people after Avery: a learner who has been through this holds knowledge the office cannot write down, which is why peer mentorship exists, why the longer tradition holds that research and practice on disability should be governed by disabled people (source 23), and why the disability justice tradition puts leadership with those most affected (source 24). The seven stops Avery followed map onto the four components the self-advocacy research names: knowledge of self, knowledge of rights, communication, and leadership (source 25). The first six stops are the first three. This stop is the fourth, and the research on learners with disabilities in post-secondary education finds that self-determination of this kind is among the things that most shape how they fare (source 26). A request handled well is the learner’s. Spoken about, it becomes the case for the next one.
