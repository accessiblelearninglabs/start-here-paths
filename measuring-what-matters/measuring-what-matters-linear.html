A request is where the work begins. Follow one through to where it changes the institution.

One accommodation request, followed from the day it reaches an advisor’s desk to the change it leaves behind in the institution. Seven stops; at each, the question the practitioner faces, the resources that answer it, and what a good decision looks like. It is a way into the landscape of seventy-three knowledge resources for anyone who would rather begin with a case than with a map.

Determinations in individual accommodation cases rest with accessibility advisors, accommodation offices and legal counsel; this path is offered in support of that work, never in place of it.

Screen reader and keyboard optimised version. All content is presented in reading order, with no tabs, frames or scripting. Every stop is a section with its own heading, so the page can be navigated by heading; the sources are numbered and each number in the text links to its entry. Three ways to read this

  • Fifteen minutes. Read the path through. It stands on its own and does not require a single link to be opened.
  • An hour. Read the path, then open the stop that matches where you are, with its resources beside it.
  • A term. Use the seven stops as a reading order through the landscape. Taken in sequence, their resources touch every group on the map.

A note on confidentiality

Rowan is not a real person. The case that follows is a composite of experiences common in post-secondary accommodation practice, written so that the reasoning behind an accommodation decision can be shown from beginning to end. No detail is drawn from a real learner, a real file or a real course.

Real accommodation records are confidential. They are held by the office that creates them, disclosed only to the extent needed to deliver the accommodation, and protected by provincial privacy legislation (source 30). That responsibility applies at all times and to everyone who touches a case: an advisor, an instructor, an invigilator, a colleague. A real case is never discussed, taught from or written about in a form that could identify the person, and the same applies to anyone who uses this path as a model.

The case

The case: Rowan, second year, a laboratory course, one term

Rowan is in the second year of a science degree. This term includes a chemistry course with a three-hour weekly laboratory, a midterm and a final exam. The laboratory component ends in a timed practical in week twelve.

Rowan lives with a chronic health condition that is episodic. In most weeks it is manageable; in a flare it is not. Rowan registered with the access office in first year and holds an accommodation letter for extended time on exams. Nothing in that letter speaks to attendance or deadlines, because nothing in first year required it.

In week two, Rowan writes to the office. The laboratory allows one missed session before marks are lost, and a flare in September has already used it. Rowan asks for three things: that missed laboratories not be penalised, that assignment deadlines be extended when a flare occurs, and that the timed practical be rescheduled or extended if a flare falls on the day. The file holds a specialist’s letter from three years ago. It names the condition and recommends “flexibility with deadlines and attendance.”

The file lands with an advisor. What follows is the work.

Stop 1 of 7

Stop 1: The frame

Where the request is now

Week two. A letter, three requests and a course outline, read before anything is decided.

The question

What is in front of me: a condition, a set of barriers, or a course design? And what is my part in it?

What to read

  • Why Universal Design Never Eliminates Individual Accommodation — the anchor of the whole landscape. Rowan’s request is not a failure of the course’s design and not an administrative residue; it is the point at which a duty owed to a person is met by skilled practice.
  • Functional Impact in Academic Tasks — starts from what the course asks each week, not from the diagnosis. Sustained standing at a bench, timed recall, deadlines across a term: this is where the condition and the course actually meet.
  • Models of Disability as Design Decisions — the model implicit in how a file is read decides what gets built. A medical reading sees only the letter; an interactional reading sees the letter meeting the laboratory.

A good decision looks like

Before anyone is written to, the request is rewritten in the file as barriers met in tasks: a fixed attendance rule meeting a condition that fluctuates; fixed deadlines meeting a condition that fluctuates; a timed practical meeting a condition that fluctuates. This is the frame the International Classification of Functioning supplies, in which functioning is the interaction of a health condition with activities, participation and the environment rather than a property of the person (source 19), and it is the frame disability studies has long insisted on: a barrier is named as a feature of the environment and the institution, not as a deficit in the learner (sources 26, 27). The condition stays in the file. The barriers go to work.

One barrier is easy to miss. The Episodic Disability Framework treats uncertainty about the future as a dimension of disability in its own right, beside symptoms, daily activities and social inclusion (source 20). Rowan’s third request, for what happens if a flare falls on the day of the practical, is a response to that uncertainty, and the course outline offers nothing to answer it.

The advisor’s part is named at the same time. The advisor decides accommodation, with the instructor and within policy. The advisor does not decide what chemistry requires. Keeping those two apart is what makes the fifth stop possible.

Stop 2 of 7

Stop 2: What is owed

Where the request is now

Week two, the same day. Before the reply to Rowan is written.

The question

What does the institution owe Rowan, what is Rowan asked to do in return, and what is the instructor’s part?

What to read

  • The Duty to Accommodate in Learning Design — the procedural duty and the substantive one, the unified test for a standard, and accommodation to the point of undue hardship, in the Canadian frame.
  • The Case Law Behind Accommodation in Education — six decisions in plain language. Three carry this stop: Renaud, which makes the search for accommodation a shared responsibility in which the person seeking it must also take part (source 2); Meiorin, which sets the test a standard must meet and places on the institution the burden of showing that further accommodation would be undue hardship (source 1); and Moore, in which the Supreme Court of Canada described the service owed as meaningful access to education (source 6).
  • How a University Accommodation Policy Works — the university’s own policy, read as the brief: who is eligible, who decides, what an instructor is responsible for, and how a disagreement is resolved.
  • The Interactive Process in Learning Settings — what good faith requires on each side, and the points at which the process stalls: no response, an unworkable offer, or a request handled as a complaint.

A good decision looks like

The reply to Rowan opens a process rather than issuing a ruling. It says what the office will do and by when, what it needs from Rowan, and that the instructor will be part of it. The duty it answers is the duty to accommodate under the provincial Human Rights Code (source 7), and it is a positive duty: equal access to a public service can require the institution to take measures, not only to refrain from excluding (source 4). The existing letter for extended time settles nothing about the three new requests. A blanket rule is no answer to a person; the duty calls for individual assessment (source 3), so each barrier is looked at on its own terms, with the focus on needs and limitations rather than on the diagnosis (source 9).

Where practice disagrees

Whether the laboratory already missed in September can be covered by an accommodation granted in week two. Accommodation after a deadline, test or course has already passed is treated in human rights guidance as a question of its own rather than as the ordinary case (source 9), and the shared responsibility that Renaud describes includes bringing a need forward in time for it to be met (source 2). This path treats the September absence under the course’s own rule and the instructor’s discretion, runs the accommodation forward from the request, and writes down why. Others would reach back. Either way, the reasoning belongs in the record.

Stop 3 of 7

Stop 3: Before anyone asks

Where the request is now

Week three. The advisor reads the course outline closely before meeting the instructor.

The question

Which of these barriers did the course’s design put there, and which are owed to Rowan individually?

What to read

  • Time, Pacing and Deadlines as Design Variables — a time limit is either part of what an assessment measures or a convention it inherited, and the two call for different responses. Generous time as a design choice does not reach every case.
  • Designing for Episodic and Fluctuating Conditions — attendance policy and deadline architecture as program design, for the conditions most systems assume are stable.
  • Designing Out the Disclosure Requirement — a flexible deadline that requires an email is a disclosure event; a built-in grace window is not.
  • Essential Requirements and the Limits of Flexibility — what a standard genuinely requires, separated from what it has inherited by convention, and articulated before any dispute rather than during one.

A good decision looks like

Three findings go into the file. The one-absence rule and the fixed deadlines are design choices. A second permitted absence and a short grace window on submissions would have met two of Rowan’s three requests with no letter, no disclosure and no advisor, and would have served the learners in the same room who will never make a request at all. Those two findings become a note for the instructor now and material for the seventh stop later.

The two findings have a place in the design framework they come from. Attendance and deadline architecture are matters of engagement in the Universal Design for Learning Guidelines; the form of the practical is a matter of action and expression (source 23). Human variability is the rule in any learning environment rather than the exception (source 24), which is why a design change made for one learner reaches many. It does not reach all: a grace window still has an edge, and an episodic condition can exceed it. Design reduces the individual duty; it does not discharge it (source 25).

The timed practical is different. It may be measuring something, and whether it is cannot be settled by the advisor alone. It is held for the fifth stop. Design fixes what it can; what it cannot fix is owed individually; and the advisor can tell the two apart.

Stop 4 of 7

Stop 4: When someone asks

Where the request is now

Week three. Rowan comes in.

The question

How is this conversation conducted so that it is safe and useful to Rowan, and what does the file actually establish?

What to read

  • The Advising Conversation — a designed encounter. Safety, transparency and choice built into its structure; barriers asked about without requiring a person to prove themselves again; the learner leaving better able to describe their own access requirements.
  • Reading Disability Documentation — what a report can establish and what it cannot; the weight professional guidance gives to the learner’s own account; the limits of a clinician’s recommendation as evidence.
  • Documentation Requirements and Medical Gatekeeping — what is actually required against what is routinely requested, and why cost and waiting time for a new assessment are barriers in their own right.
  • Responding to Distress and Making a Referral — held in reserve. If the conversation turns, the pathway was designed in advance and the person in the room knows the limits of their role.
  • Intersectionality in Accommodation: Who Reaches the Office — the learners who are not in this room, and why. Rowan found the office; the conversation is conducted knowing that many do not.

A good decision looks like

The conversation is built on the principles of a trauma-informed approach: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment, voice and choice; and attention to the cultural, historical and gender context the person brings (source 21). In practice that means Rowan is told at the outset what will be asked, why, who will see it and what happens next, and is not asked to establish the condition again.

The three-year-old letter is read for what it establishes, that the condition is ongoing and episodic, and not for what it cannot, which is how a flare meets this laboratory in this term. Professional guidance treats the learner as a primary source of information about barriers and about which accommodations have and have not worked, and holds that many requests can be decided with little outside documentation (source 16). Rowan’s own account does that work: what a flare does to standing at a bench, to reading, to timed tasks, and how long one lasts. Weighed for its clarity and consistency with the letter and the course outline, that account is recorded as the functional picture of the case.

The notes record the process, not the diagnosis repeated: the date, what was asked, what was offered, what Rowan chose, what was declined and why, who may see it. If distress surfaces in the room, the pathway was designed in advance, the person present knows the limits of a non-clinical role, and a follow-up with Rowan is arranged (source 22).

Where practice disagrees

Whether Rowan should be sent for updated documentation. Many offices would require it, and a letter that is silent on attendance and deadlines is, on its face, limited in scope. This path follows the guidance: third-party documentation is sought when the learner cannot explain how the disability creates the barrier and how the requested accommodation would address it, and blanket age limits on documentation are not applied (source 16). Rowan can explain both. A recommendation of “flexibility” does not need a clinician to translate it into an attendance rule; the advisor and the learner do that together, and the record says why no further documentation was required.

Stop 5 of 7

Stop 5: The hard call

Where the request is now

Week four. The instructor replies that the practical is timed because speed is part of laboratory competence: reactions have to be run within a window, the room is booked for three hours, and a different day means a different practical.

The question

Is time part of what the practical measures, or a convention it inherited? And what accommodation preserves the measurement?

What to read

  • Essential Requirements and the Limits of Flexibility — the most demanding judgement in the field: when flexibility preserves what is being measured, and when it alters it.
  • Accommodated Exams and Assessment Logistics — changing the conditions of an assessment without changing what it measures; how an accommodation carries into a laboratory exam; what the research on extended time does and does not show; what happens when a person’s health fails during the exam itself.
  • Reasoning Through an Accommodation Request — invented cases worked from first contact to a recorded decision, and the record each decision leaves: what is written down, what is kept out, who may see it.
  • Access in Studio, Shop and Lab-Based Learning — protecting the safety requirements that are genuine while examining the ones that are only habit.
  • Eliminating Construct-Irrelevant Barriers in Assessment — the validity principle underneath all of it: a result should reflect capability, not incidental obstacles.

A good decision looks like

The advisor does not decide what chemistry requires, and does not accept the instructor’s first answer either. Validity belongs to the interpretation of a score, not to the test itself (source 10), so the question is what the practical’s score is taken to mean. Put in plain words, as Meiorin asks it: which parts of the practical are timed because the chemistry is timed, and which because the room is booked? Asked that way, the instructor separates them. Two of the six tasks are time-critical; a reaction has to be quenched inside a short window or the result is meaningless. The three-hour total is the booking. The first is a bona fide requirement of the standard; the second is construct-irrelevant variance, a feature of the test that affects the score without being part of what is measured (sources 1, 11).

The decision follows the line that answer draws. The two time-critical tasks keep the pace the chemistry sets, for every learner. The session as a whole is extended by the ratio already in Rowan’s letter, and that ratio is itself examined rather than inherited: a pan-Canadian study found the common 150 per cent allowance usually set at intake from policy or custom rather than from evidence about the person (source 15), and the research on extended time supports individual decisions over standing formulas (source 14). A deferred sitting is arranged in advance in case a flare falls on the day, with a stated rule for who calls it and by when, so that nobody is deciding in the corridor. The record carries the reasoning: what was essential, how that was established and by whom, what was offered, what was declined and why. A decision reasoned this way is an argument that links the performance to the inference drawn from it (source 12).

Where practice disagrees

Whether a deferred practical is still the same assessment. Comparability across conditions of administration is something to be argued, not asserted: the deferred sitting samples the same content, sets the same tasks at the same pace, and is no easier or harder (sources 10, 13). This path holds that a practical meeting those conditions is the same assessment, and the record says so in those terms. What remains is effort. Effort can choose between two accommodations that both work; it cannot defeat the only one that does, short of undue hardship (source 5), and effort is a question for the sixth stop.

Stop 6 of 7

Stop 6: Making it real

Where the request is now

Weeks five to twelve. The letter is issued. Now it has to happen.

The question

An accommodation has been approved. What has to occur, and who has to do it, for it to exist on the day?

What to read

  • Working with Accommodation Offices and Access Advisors — the implementation gap, in which an approved accommodation is recorded and then not delivered, and what a referral has to carry to be usable.
  • Accommodated Exams and Assessment Logistics — this time for its second half: booking, invigilator training, communication with learners and instructors, and the decisions required when timing, technology or a person’s health fails during an exam.
  • Delivering Accommodated Exams in a Learning Platform — the midterm is online. Platform time settings, lockdown browsers and proctoring can conflict with a learner’s set-up; a test routine run in advance catches it.
  • Interpreting, Live Captioning, Note-Taking and Alternate Formats — not needed in Rowan’s case, and read anyway, because the same lesson applies: a service arranged late, or without the materials, may not provide the access it was approved to provide.
  • Funding Routes for Disability-Related Supports and Equipment — for the cases where an approved accommodation depends on something the learner has to pay for.
  • Training and Supervising Student Access Staff — the invigilator or laboratory aide who is in the room on the day, and what they need to be able to do on a first shift.

A good decision looks like

Delivery began before the letter. Between the request in week two and the letter in week five, Rowan was told in writing what had been agreed so far and what was still open, and the deadline arrangement was applied in the meantime; an accommodation process that leaves a person without an answer while it runs is one of the ways the process stalls (source 2).

Every line of the letter has an owner, a date and a check. The instructor confirms the deadline arrangement on the course site, so Rowan does not have to ask twice. The exam service books the midterm with extended time and runs the platform check with Rowan’s own set-up two weeks out, because conformance to the accessibility guidelines is defined against the assistive technology a person actually uses, and a locked-down browser that blocks it is a failure of the exam, not of the learner (sources 28, 29). The laboratory coordinator holds the deferred slot, and the teaching assistant on the day of the practical knows the rule for calling it. In week seven the advisor checks that the midterm happened as approved, and the file is not closed until each line has been delivered or declined with a reason. An accommodation that is recorded and not delivered has discharged nothing (source 17).

Stop 7 of 7

Stop 7: Changing the institution

Where the request is now

December. The term is over. Rowan sat the practical on the scheduled day, used one deferred deadline, and missed no further laboratories.

The question

What did this one request show about the course, the service and the institution, and what changes so that the next learner does not have to ask?

What to read

  • Faculty Development That Changes Accessible Practice — whether the gap is capability, opportunity or motivation, and the orientation, guidance and point-of-need supports through which an access service explains itself each term.
  • Designing for Episodic and Fluctuating Conditions — read again, now as the design brief for the course outline the instructor writes next year.
  • Professional Standards for Disability Services — the CAS and AHEAD standards read as evaluation criteria, and how a self-study against them is designed.
  • Evaluating Whether Design Reduced Barriers — measuring whether a design change lowered barriers rather than assuming it did.
  • Access at Admission and Transition and Peer Mentorship as Access Infrastructure — the interval before the system knows a learner, and the middle ground between a learner and an office where most of the daily work of access happens.
  • Institutional Accessibility Planning as a Change Program — where the pattern across cases is taken, and what turns a published commitment into changed practice.

A good decision looks like

Three things leave the file. For the instructor: a note that a second permitted absence and a two-day grace window would have met two of the three requests with no letter at all, and that the essential-requirements reasoning written for the practical now exists and belongs in next year’s course outline and in the program’s published requirements, where every learner can read it. The note is about the design of the course; it is not an evaluation of the person who taught it.

For the service: the case is counted. How many requests this term were an attendance rule or a deadline policy meeting an episodic condition? The count shows how often; Rowan’s file shows why. Both are evidence, and the professional standards for a disability service ask it to gather exactly this kind of evidence about its own practice and to act on it (sources 17, 18).

For the institution: the count goes to the accessibility plan with a proposal and a named sponsor. The provincial legislation requires the plan, a committee and a feedback mechanism (source 8); ownership and sequencing are what the proposal adds. Next year the service measures two things, not one: whether the outline changed, and whether the requests fell. A fall in requests without the first check proves little, and a change in the count can have more than one cause. Measured this way, a request handled well becomes evidence, and evidence counted across cases becomes the case for the next change.
