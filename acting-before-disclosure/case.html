A note on confidentiality

Jules is not a real person. The case that follows is a composite of experiences common in large first-year courses, written so that the reasoning behind an instructor’s decisions can be shown from beginning to end. No detail is drawn from a real learner, a real file, or a real course.

A learner who writes to an instructor about their health has disclosed something confidential. What they wrote is held by the person it was sent to, shared only as far as arranging what was asked for requires, and protected by provincial privacy legislation (source 7). An instructor does not forward it to a teaching assistant, raise it in a department meeting, or keep it in a place where others can read it. A real case is never discussed, taught from, or written about in a form that could identify the person.

The case

Jules, first year, a three-hundred-seat course, week nine

The course is a first-year introduction to earth science: two lectures a week in a large hall, a weekly tutorial run by teaching assistants, two written assignments, a final exam, and a mandatory field day in week eleven, held on a Saturday at a site two hours from campus. Tutorial participation is worth ten per cent, assessed by attendance and contribution. The lecture hall records every lecture; by department custom, recordings are released only to learners who hold an accommodation letter that names them.

In week seven, something changed for Jules. Concentrating through a fifty-minute lecture became difficult, mornings became unreliable, and a crowded tutorial room became hard to stay in. Jules missed both tutorials in weeks seven and eight and has not started the second assignment, due in week nine. Jules has never needed to ask for anything before and is not registered with the access office.

On the Monday of week nine, at eleven at night, Jules writes to the instructor. The email says that there is a health reason, which Jules does not name, that the last two weeks have been bad, and asks whether the assignment can be extended, whether the lecture recordings can be shared, and whether the missed tutorials will count. It ends: “I don’t know if this is something you can help with or if I need to go somewhere else.”

Two weeks later, in week eleven, an accommodation letter arrives from the access office. It provides extended time on examinations, flexibility with deadlines, access to lecture recordings, and asks the instructor to consider the effect of a health-related absence on attendance-based components. The field day is that Saturday. Jules has written again to say that a full day at a crowded site, with the travel, is not possible this week.

The email lands with the instructor. What follows is the work.
