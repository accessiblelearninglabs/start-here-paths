1The frame

Week nine, Tuesday morning. The email, read before anything is decided.

The questionWhat is in front of me: an email, a set of barriers, or a course design? And which of these can I act on today, on my own authority?
What to read
  • Why Universal Design Never Eliminates Individual Accommodation — the anchor of the whole landscape. Jules’s email is not an interruption of the course and not a matter for someone else; it is the point at which a duty owed to a person is met by the person who happens to be nearest.
  • Working with Accommodation Offices and Access Advisors — what an accommodation office decides, what an instructor decides, and what a referral has to carry to be usable. The line between the two is the first thing to find.
  • Functional Impact in Academic Tasks — starts from what the course asks each week, not from the diagnosis. A tutorial that assesses presence, an assignment with a fixed date, a recorded lecture that cannot be watched: this is where the course meets what has changed for Jules.
  • Designing Out the Disclosure Requirement — Jules had to describe a private health matter to an instructor at eleven at night to ask for a recording that already exists. That is a disclosure event, and the course put it there.
A good decision looks like

Before replying, the instructor rewrites the email as barriers met in tasks: a fixed deadline meeting a capacity that has changed; a participation mark awarded for presence meeting two weeks of absence; a recording withheld by custom meeting a learner who needs it. This is the frame the International Classification of Functioning supplies, in which functioning is the interaction of a health condition with activities, participation and the environment rather than a property of the person (source 17), and it is the frame disability studies has long insisted on: a barrier is a feature of the environment and the institution, not a deficit in the learner (sources 20, 21). What changed is Jules’s to hold. The barriers are the course’s.

Then the instructor sorts the barriers by who can remove them. The deadline is the instructor’s: the course outline permits extensions at the instructor’s discretion, and no policy requires a letter before one is granted. The tutorial marks are the instructor’s, through the teaching assistants. The recordings are the instructor’s, whatever the custom. The final exam is not: its conditions are set through the access office and the exam service, and that is the referral the reply will carry. Three of four barriers can be acted on today, by the person reading the email.

Where practice disagreesWhether an instructor should do anything at all before a letter exists. Many departments tell instructors to wait for the office, on the reasonable ground that instructors are not trained to assess disability and should not try. This path agrees with the ground and not with the conclusion. Extending a deadline, releasing a recording and handling an absence under the course’s own rules are not assessments of disability; they are the ordinary exercise of an instructor’s authority over their own course, available to any learner who asks. Human rights guidance treats interim accommodation, while a formal process runs, as part of the duty rather than a courtesy (source 8), and the process the Supreme Court of Canada described in Renaud is a shared one in which each party does what is within its reach (source 1). What the instructor must not do is decide what Jules is entitled to under policy. That is the office’s. What the instructor can do is make the next two weeks survivable while the office works.
