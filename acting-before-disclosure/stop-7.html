7Changing the institution

December. The term is over. Jules submitted both assignments, completed the field component at the alternative site, and sat the final with extended time.

The questionWhat did this one email show about the course, the department, and the institution, and what changes so that the next learner does not have to write at eleven at night?
What to read
  • Designing for Episodic and Fluctuating Conditions — read again, now as the design brief for the course outline the instructor writes next year.
  • Faculty Development That Changes Accessible Practice — whether the gap across a department is capability, opportunity, or motivation, and the orientation and point-of-need supports that reach instructors where they are.
  • Evaluating Whether Design Reduced Barriers — measuring whether a design change lowered barriers rather than assuming it did.
  • Access at Admission and Transition — the first term, before the system knows a learner. Jules was in week nine of a first year and had never registered anywhere; the interval before the system knows a person is where this case began.
  • Professional Standards for Disability Services — the standards against which a service evaluates its own timeliness, read here for the two weeks between the email and the letter.
  • Institutional Accessibility Planning as a Change Program — where the pattern across courses is taken, and what turns a published commitment into changed practice.
A good decision looks like

Three things leave the instructor’s desk. For the course: next year’s outline releases every recording to every learner, with captions; measures participation by contribution across the term rather than presence on the day; builds a three-day grace window into each assignment; and states, in the outline where every learner can read it, what the field component measures and that an alternative visit exists for anyone who cannot attend the scheduled day. The essential-requirements reasoning written in week eleven now exists, and it belongs in the outline rather than in an email thread.

For the department and the service: the instructor tells the access office what the two weeks before the letter were like, and what would have met the request without one. A service measures itself against professional standards that include timeliness and the evaluation of its own practice (sources 15, 16), and an instructor’s account of the gap is evidence it needs. The proposal that goes with it is a service standard for that gap: a response within a stated number of days, an interim arrangement named in the first reply, and written reasons. The department receives the same note as a design question for every large first-year course: which outlines release recordings, which assess presence, and which say what their field or laboratory components measure.

For the institution: the case is counted, and the count goes to the accessibility plan with a named sponsor. The provincial legislation requires the plan, a committee, and a feedback mechanism (source 10); ownership and sequencing are what the proposal adds. In the most recent national survey, one in five Canadians aged fifteen to twenty-four reported a disability (source 26); a first-year course of three hundred cannot assume otherwise of its hall. Next year the department measures two things, not one: whether the outlines changed, and whether learners in those courses had to ask. Measured that way, an email answered well becomes evidence, and evidence counted across courses becomes the case for the next change.
